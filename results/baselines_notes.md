# Phase 2 baselines — how to read these numbers

Generated by `python -m eval.run_sts`. Metric is Spearman rank correlation ×100
between cosine similarity and the human gold score. Higher is better.

| model | STS12 | STS13 | STS14 | STS15 | STS16 | STS-B | Avg |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| glove-avg | 57.25 | 53.79 | 52.00 | 59.70 | 49.83 | 47.37 | **53.32** |
| bert-mean | 30.87 | 59.89 | 47.73 | 60.29 | 63.73 | 47.29 | **51.63** |
| bert-cls | 21.54 | 32.11 | 21.28 | 37.89 | 44.24 | 20.30 | **29.56** |

## The headline result

**Averaged GloVe (53.32) beats mean-pooled BERT (51.63).** A 2014 word-vector
average outperforms a 2018 transformer at judging sentence similarity. This
reproduces the central motivation of Sentence-BERT: BERT was never trained to make
its output vectors comparable by cosine distance, so using them that way is a
misuse of the model rather than a limitation of it.

`[CLS]` pooling collapses to 29.56 — close to the 29.19 the paper reports — despite
being the vector most often assumed to be "the sentence embedding".

## Two methodological choices, measured rather than assumed

**1. Pooled Spearman, not per-subset averaged.** Following the SBERT paper, one
Spearman is computed over all pairs in a dataset. SentEval instead scores each
sub-dataset separately and averages. The difference is not cosmetic — measured on
STS-B with GloVe:

| | |
|---|---|
| pooled over all 1,379 pairs | 44.11 |
| per-subset average | 50.07 |

The subsets have very different score distributions (answer-answer 23.43, MSRvid
68.55), so pooling them depresses the correlation. **Any comparison against a
published number must first establish which of these two it used.**

**2. GloVe lookups are lowercased.** GloVe 840B is case-sensitive and holds separate
vectors for "The" and "the". Preferring the cased form made sentence-initial words
use a different vector from the same word mid-sentence. Cost of getting this
backwards: **4.1 points on STS-B** (44.11 → 48.21 in isolation). Fixed by looking up
the lowercased token first. Baselines are kept as strong as honestly possible — a
weak baseline would make Phase 3's improvement look larger than it is.

## Why the absolute numbers sit below commonly-cited figures

Our GloVe average (53.32) and BERT-mean average (51.63) are a few points below the
~58 / ~55 usually quoted. Known contributors, in order of measured size:

1. **Aggregation** — worth ~6 points on STS-B alone (above).
2. **Averaging 6 datasets, not 7.** The paper's Avg. column includes SICK-R, which
   we do not run.
3. **Source preprocessing.** `mteb`'s STS12–16 are lowercased with spaced
   punctuation ("aren 't"); STS-B keeps its original casing.

What is *not* the cause, checked explicitly: the GloVe parse is correct
(cos(king,queen)=0.725, cos(king,paris)=0.278, 300 dimensions, <1% OOV on every
dataset), and the harness carries no leakage (a random encoder scores −1.63).

**The full side-by-side against the paper's Table 1 belongs in Phase 3**, where the
brief requires it and where the paper's per-dataset figures should be read off the
paper rather than recalled.

## Sanity gates this run passed

- A random encoder scores −1.63 on STS-B — no leakage in the harness.
- `[CLS]` lands far below both other methods, as the paper reports.
- GloVe beats BERT. Had BERT won, the first suspects would have been attention-mask
  pooling and cosine normalisation, not a genuine discovery.

# Review retrieval baselines

26 hand-labelled queries, 721 judgements, 390 marked relevant (median 16 per
query). Retrieval runs over all 45,864 distinct review texts.

| model | Recall@10 | Precision@10 | MRR |
|---|---|---|---|
| tfidf | **46.01** | **65.00** | **85.71** |
| glove-avg | 37.43 | 58.85 | 78.22 |
| bert-mean | 27.88 | 43.46 | 74.10 |

Maximum achievable Recall@10 is **71.84**, not 100. With a median of 16 relevant
reviews per query you cannot fit them all into a top-10, so the ceiling is
`min(10, n_relevant) / n_relevant`. TF-IDF's 46.01 is therefore **64% of what is
achievable**, not 46% of perfect.

## The headline: TF-IDF beats both embedding baselines

Plain word-overlap search wins on this data, and mean-pooled BERT comes last —
the same ordering as the STS results, on a completely different task. Raw BERT
embeddings are not merely unimpressive at sentence similarity; they are worse than
counting words.

**This sets the bar Phases 3 and 4 must clear: 46.01 Recall@10 and 65.00
Precision@10.** It also means "semantic search" is not automatically better than
keyword search here — the project has to earn that claim rather than assume it.

## Which metric to trust

**Precision@10 separates the models best** (65.00 / 58.85 / 43.46). **MRR
saturates** — with 16 relevant reviews per query, any competent system lands one
near rank 1, so all three score 74-86 and the metric barely discriminates. Report
MRR for completeness, but do not lead with it.

## Bias in this evaluation, stated plainly

The pool was built from these same three systems, so each contributed its own
top-10 and each gets credit for candidates it surfaced. The bias is roughly
symmetric between them, so the *ordering* is meaningful.

It is **not** symmetric for the Phase 3/4 models, which could not contribute to a
pool built before they existed. They will surface relevant reviews nobody labelled,
which score as misses. Any improvement they show is therefore a **lower bound**.
If a trained model beats TF-IDF on this eval set, it beat it with a handicap.

Queries were written as natural language rather than the mined corpus phrases, so
TF-IDF is not being handed its own search terms — "the food was cold by the time
it arrived" rather than "food cold".
